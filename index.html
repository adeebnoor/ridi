Computational decision systems · biomedical AI · responsible evaluation

From knowledge
to decision identity.

We study a single question across computational systems: what information must be observed, represented and reported for a decision to be scientifically identifiable?

The research programme connects biomedical knowledge integration, drug–drug interaction discovery, mechanistic explanation, interpretable AI, and the evaluation of capacity-limited decision systems. RIDI is the current flagship project: it asks who actually receives finite action when AI scores become decisions.

One research arc, not disconnected projects.

The programme has progressively moved from making fragmented biomedical knowledge computable, to explaining inferred interactions, to evaluating what modern predictive systems reveal—and what they leave unidentified at the point of action.

Flagship project · RIDI

Same audit. Different identities.

A system can pass the same reported aggregate audit while acting on different entities.

RIDI studies allocation identity: when a model, ranking, or scoring system has finite capacity, which people, cases, alerts, vulnerabilities, documents, or institutions actually receive action—and which identities change across versions?

Work with the lab.

We are interested in collaborations where computational scores become scarce real-world attention or action. The strongest projects may confirm the phenomenon, delimit it, or show that a different allocation-aware formulation is better.

Bring a system

Clinical alerts, cybersecurity, fraud, inspection, hiring, grants, document triage, or another finite-action pipeline.

Bring a scientific challenge

Build the next method

Principal investigator

Adeeb Noor

Professor, Department of Information Technology, Faculty of Computing and Information Technology, King Abdulaziz University, Jeddah, Saudi Arabia.

His research spans computational drug safety, semantic technologies, biomedical knowledge integration, interpretable AI, healthcare digital transformation, and decision-system evaluation. He previously served as a visiting professor with the Bio-Ontology Research Group at KAUST.

The intellectual thread began with doctoral work on the D3 Pharmacovigilance System and now extends to a broader question of informational completeness in computational decisions.
